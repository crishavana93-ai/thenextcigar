## Caption (paste, WITH-BRANDS version)

Twin Names, part 3: the court case that split the names.

• 15 September 1960: Cuba takes over five cigar companies and puts in state managers, called "interventors". The owners leave their factories for the last time.
• The cigars keep going to the US under the same names. So who owns the names? And who gets paid?
• 1972: a federal judge in New York rules on both questions.
  - Money for cigars shipped before the takeover belongs to the exiled owners; the court says a debt sits where the debtor is, in the US.
  - Money for cigars shipped after goes to Cuba's interventors.
  - In the US, Cuba's takeover didn't take the names from the exiled owners.
• The twist: the owners win no damages. The court found they couldn't show any loss.
• 1976: the money fight reaches the US Supreme Court. By five votes to four, refusing to repay an importer's mistaken payments was not an act of state, so the importer could collect.

Cuba kept the factories. But in the US, the takeover never reached the names.

Trademarks named in the 1972 case: H. Upmann, Monte Cristo, Partagas, Ramon Allones, Por Larranaga, Hoyo de Monterey, Belinda and Punch (the case's spellings).

Next: the name that was never seized. History, not advice. We don't sell tobacco.

Sources: Menendez v. Faber, Coe & Gregg, 345 F.Supp. 527 (S.D.N.Y. 1972), affirmed in part, 485 F.2d 1355 (2d Cir. 1973); US Supreme Court, 425 U.S. 682 (1976).

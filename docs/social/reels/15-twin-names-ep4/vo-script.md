Part four. The name that was never seized. In 1960, Cuba took over its leading cigar makers. But one name, Cuba never had to take, because Cuba made it. Cohiba.

Fidel Castro told the story to Cigar Aficionado in 1994. A bodyguard knew a man who made cigars Castro liked. So, he said, "we set up the house." That house became El Laguito, a factory in Havana.

Who rolled the first one? Even Cigar Aficionado has printed two versions: Avelino Lara, in 1968, or Eduardo Rivera, in the early 1960s, with the name first used in 1966.

For years, Cohibas were best known as official gifts, for heads of state and diplomats, and were sold only inside Cuba. The world launch came in 1982.

Meanwhile, in December 1977, inside an American cigar company, General Cigar, notes on its internal memos called it Castro's brand cigar. Three months later, the company applied for the name in the US. It was registered in 1981. In November 1992, as the cigar boom began, General relaunched its own Cohiba.

In 1997, Cuba's state cigar company fought back: first at the US trademark board, then in a New York court. In 2004, a New York judge sided with Cuba. But in 2005, an appeals court said the embargo blocked Cuba's claim. And the US Supreme Court declined to hear it.

So Cuba pressed on at the trademark board, with a treaty from 1929, the Pan American Convention. In 2014, a federal appeals court said Cuba's treaty claim could go ahead at the board. In 2022, the board ordered the American registrations cancelled. In 2025, a federal judge in Virginia reviewed the case afresh and reached the same result.

The twist: Cuban Cohibas still can't be sold in the US. And the American Cohiba is still on sale. In September 2026, three appeals judges heard the case. Their ruling is still pending.

# The Next Cigar — accessories business plan

**Prepared 11 September 2026 for Cristian Ortiz Suárez · Sweden (enskild firma)**
Scope: drop-shipped and lightly stocked cigar accessories and clothing sold through thenextcigar.com. No tobacco products.

A note before anything else: I am not a lawyer or an accountant, and several things below turn on Swedish tax rules and EU product law where a professional's sign-off is worth more than my research. Where a fact is confirmed against an official source I say so; where it is not, I say that too, and those are the points to take to a professional rather than to me.

---

## 1. The honest strategic read

The thing worth being clear-eyed about first: selling Chinese-made cigar cutters online is not a business with a moat. Anyone can open the same CJ Dropshipping catalogue, anyone can list the same guillotine cutter, and the people who win at that game win on ad spend and volume, not on taste. If the plan is "become a drop-shipper," you are entering a commodity market against people who have been optimising it for years and who do not care about cigars.

What you have that they do not is the rest of the site. An audience arriving for the Finder, the Habanos Index, the landed-cost estimator and the Lounge is an audience that already trusts your judgement about Cuban cigars. That trust is the asset. The shop is a way to convert it, and it will work in proportion to how much it feels like an extension of the editorial rather than a bolt-on store.

That has one concrete consequence for every decision below: **curation beats catalogue.** Eight things you have handled, photographed yourself and can describe honestly will outsell eighty listings scraped from a supplier. It is also the only version of this that is defensible if a competitor shows up, and the only version that doesn't quietly damage the editorial credibility that makes the rest of the site work.

## 2. What can actually be sold — the product decision

The research turned up two hard constraints that reshape the product list before any commercial question is asked.

**Butane lighters are close to undeliverable by post.** A gas-filled lighter is UN Class 2.1 dangerous goods. PostNord's own list of dangerous goods permitted in letter post does not include lighters, and the consistent position across carriers is that they may not travel in ordinary international air parcel post. This was the single most important finding: a drop-shipped butane lighter from a Chinese supplier to a Swedish customer is very likely to be seized or returned, and neither you nor the supplier will be told why in any useful timeframe. If you want to sell flame, the realistic routes are sea freight into EU stock with proper dangerous-goods declarations, or buying from an EU-based distributor who has already done that. Electric-arc and USB lighters avoid the gas problem but bring WEEE and battery producer-registration obligations in Sweden instead. There is no free option here; there is only choosing which compliance you take on.

**Lighters also carry a child-resistance rule.** Commission Decision 2006/502/EC requires that only child-resistant lighters be placed on the EU market and bans novelty lighters outright — the ones shaped like toys, animals, food. A good deal of cheap Chinese lighter stock fails one or both tests. This has to be checked per SKU, with documentation from the supplier, not assumed.

So the product list that survives contact with the rules, in the order I would launch it:

**Tier one — start here.** Cutters (guillotine, V-cut, punch), cigar cases and travel cases, ashtrays, Boveda-style humidity packs and hygrometers, cigar rests, ash trays, cedar spills, cutting mats. All of it is inert, postable, unregulated beyond general product safety, and all of it is genuinely useful to the reader you already have.

**Tier two — once tier one is running.** Clothing, which for you means the Guatabey guayaberas. Textiles are simple to ship and the brand story is yours rather than a supplier's. See section 6 on the origin question, which has to be settled before a single shirt is listed.

**Tier three — only with EU stock, or not at all.** Butane lighters and torch lighters. My recommendation is that you do not launch with these, and that when you do, you buy them from a European distributor holding stock rather than drop-shipping them. The margin will be worse. The alternative is parcels that vanish.

**Not at all.** Anything humidor-electronic with a lithium battery until the WEEE and battery registration is actually in place, and any lighter you cannot get a child-resistance declaration for.

## 3. What a sale actually costs you

The biggest change to drop-shipping economics in years happened ten weeks ago, and it has to be in every price you set.

The EU has removed the €150 customs-duty exemption. From **1 July 2026** a temporary flat customs duty of **€3 per item** applies to consignments valued at €150 or under arriving from outside the EU, and it runs until **1 July 2028**, when product-specific rates take over through the new Customs Data Hub. It is per item, not per parcel: a customer who orders a cutter and a case in one order generates €6, not €3. (Sources: European Commission, taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu, 13 November 2025 and 8 June 2026.)

On a €12 cutter that is a 25% cost increase appearing from nowhere, and it lands hardest on exactly the cheap-item, direct-from-China model. It is the strongest single argument for the hybrid you already proposed — buying five of each and shipping from Sweden — because stock imported in bulk on one customs entry does not pay €3 per item at retail.

Here is the shape of the arithmetic. **The supplier costs below are placeholders until you have real quotes — replace them before you price anything.**

| | Drop-ship from China | Your stock, shipped from Sweden |
|---|---|---|
| Customer pays (incl. 25% VAT) | €29.00 | €29.00 |
| VAT out | €5.80 | €5.80 |
| Net revenue | €23.20 | €23.20 |
| Goods cost | €6.00 | €5.00 (bulk) |
| Inbound freight | included | €0.80 (apportioned) |
| Customs duty | €3.00 flat, per item | ~€0.30 (ad valorem, per item) |
| Outbound shipping | €3.50 | €4.50 (PostNord) |
| Payment fees (~2.5% + €0.25) | €0.98 | €0.98 |
| **Contribution per unit** | **€9.72** | **€11.62** |
| Delivery time | 12–25 days | 1–3 days |

The stocked column wins on money *and* on the thing that actually decides whether someone buys from you twice. A fortnight's wait for a cutter from an unnamed sender is the experience that makes a reader decide your shop is a dropshipping site with a magazine attached, rather than the other way round.

Two costs the table does not show and that kill more small shops than any of the above. The first is returns: EU consumers have an unconditional 14-day right of withdrawal on distance sales, and while the customer pays return postage **by default**, that default only holds if you disclosed it clearly before purchase — if you didn't, the cost is yours. On low-value items the practical answer the whole industry uses is to refund without demanding the return, because shipping a €12 cutter back to Shenzhen costs more than the cutter. Budget two to four per cent of revenue for it and stop worrying about it. The second is your own time: photography, descriptions, customer email. On eight products that is manageable. On eighty it is a job.

## 4. Tax and registration, Sweden and the EU

**VAT.** The Swedish standard rate is 25% and applies to everything in this catalogue (confirmed, Skatteverket). There is a domestic exemption for very small turnover, raised to SEK 120,000 a year from 1 January 2025 — but treat it as irrelevant here, because it does not help with imports and does not survive cross-border selling. Register for moms alongside your existing F-skatt.

**Selling to the rest of the EU.** Below €10,000 of cross-border B2C sales a year, you charge Swedish VAT and do nothing else. Above it, you must charge the customer's own country's rate, and the One Stop Shop lets you declare all of it on a single Swedish return instead of registering in each country (confirmed, European Commission). Register for OSS *before* you cross the threshold rather than after — the day you cross it is not a day you want to discover the paperwork. One flag: a source suggested the €10,000 threshold may change in January 2027. I could not verify it and it should be checked before you build a plan around the current number.

**Imports.** Import VAT is due on everything from outside the EU regardless of value. For consignments of €150 or under, IOSS lets you charge the destination VAT at checkout and remit monthly, rather than having the courier ambush your customer with a bill at the door. If you drop-ship at all, IOSS is not optional in practice — a customer charged again on delivery is a customer lost and a chargeback opened.

**Customs duty rates.** I could not verify the specific TARIC rates for lighters (HS 9613), cigar cutters, or shirts (HS 6205), and I am not going to guess percentages you would then price against. Worse, the correct heading for a cigar cutter is genuinely ambiguous — 8214 versus 8213 versus 8211 depending on its construction. Get the rates from Tullverket's tariff tool, and if you end up importing cutters in any volume, a Binding Tariff Information ruling settles the classification permanently and for free.

**Packaging producer responsibility.** Anyone supplying packaging into Sweden for the first time is a producer and must register with Naturvårdsverket. **There is no small-business exemption from the obligation** — under one tonne a year exempts you only from the SEK 1,250 supervision fee, not from registering and reporting by 31 March each year (confirmed, Naturvårdsverket). Do this at the start; it is cheap and it is the sort of thing that is unpleasant to discover you have skipped for two years.

**Product safety.** The General Product Safety Regulation has applied since 13 December 2024 and requires a named, contactable responsible person established in the EU for goods placed on the EU market, with the details available to the customer. For stock you import yourself, you are the importer and the responsible person. For goods shipped direct from a Chinese supplier to an EU consumer, a named EU responsible person is still required — which is another quiet argument for holding stock.

## 5. The supply model

Your own instinct — five units of each in stock, drop-ship behind it — is the right architecture, and the €3-per-item duty has made it more right since you proposed it. I would sharpen it slightly.

Hold stock of the whole tier-one list. These are small, light, non-perishable, and the entire catalogue at five units each is a few hundred euros of inventory sitting in a box at home. In exchange you get next-day dispatch, real photographs of the actual object, one customs entry instead of one per order, and you as your own GPSR responsible person. There is no version of this where drop-shipping a €12 cutter is better.

Use drop-shipping for what it is actually good at: testing. A new product goes up as drop-ship only, and if it sells five in a month it joins the stocked list. That way you never buy fifty of something nobody wanted, and no customer waits three weeks for something you sell regularly.

On CJ Dropshipping specifically, your own condition was that quality has to match the best Chinese-made equivalents. Hold that line by ordering samples of everything to yourself before it goes on the site. A cutter that crushes instead of cuts will cost you a reader, and readers are the expensive thing here. Ask every supplier for a CE/child-resistance declaration where relevant and for the manufacturer's actual name and address — you need both for GPSR, and a supplier who cannot produce them is telling you something.

Worth looking at seriously as an alternative to Chinese sourcing for part of the range: European distributors. Quality Importers and the Boveda wholesale route you already have open are worth answering, because a European or US distributor account gets you branded goods — the names your readers already know — at trade prices, with paperwork that exists, and without the dangerous-goods problem on lighters. Margin is thinner than Alibaba. Everything else is better.

## 6. The Cuba question — this one is now answerable

You asked to settle whether Guatabey can be sold as "made in Cuba" before launch. Part of it is now settled.

**Stripe's own restricted-businesses page names Cuba explicitly.** It prohibits "persons located in, resident in, or a citizen of, or products or services originating from" a list of jurisdictions, and Cuba is on it. That is Stripe's contractual policy and it binds you as a Swedish merchant, because you agreed to it — not because Swedish law says so. The practical risk is not a fine; it is a frozen account and held funds at the worst possible moment, which for a small shop is a business-ending event.

The legal position is the opposite and does not rescue you. The EU does not embargo Cuba, and the EU Blocking Statute exists precisely to stop European companies from voluntarily applying US Cuba sanctions logic to themselves. So you would be in the position of being legally permitted to sell the shirts, contractually barred by your processor from doing so, and in a grey area if you comply with the processor too enthusiastically. This is a genuine tangle and it is a lawyer's question, not mine.

My practical recommendation, which you are free to reject:

If the shirts are genuinely made in Cuba and that is the point of the brand, then Stripe is the wrong processor and you need to find one that will take it in writing before you list anything. Ask Stripe support the direct question — "I am a Swedish merchant selling cotton guayabera shirts manufactured in Cuba; is this permitted under your restricted businesses policy?" — and keep the written answer. Do not launch on a verbal or an assumption.

If the shirts are not actually manufactured in Cuba, then the whole problem evaporates, and the rule from your own editorial standards applies to your shop as much as to your articles: say where they are made. "Cuban-style guayabera" is honest and sells fine. "Made in Cuba" when they are made elsewhere is the one thing that would genuinely damage a publication whose entire proposition is that it tells the truth about Cuban cigars.

## 7. The first ninety days

**Weeks 1–2, paperwork.** Register for moms. Register with Naturvårdsverket for packaging. Open or confirm the payment processor, with the Cuba question answered in writing if guayaberas are in the launch. Decide the eight to ten launch SKUs. Order samples of all of them.

**Weeks 3–4, the goods.** Handle every sample. Reject anything you would not use. Photograph what survives, yourself, on paper, in daylight — the same visual language as the newspaper, not supplier stock photos on white. Write the descriptions the way you write the Finder: what it is, what it does well, what it doesn't. Place the first bulk order of five to ten units per surviving SKU.

**Weeks 5–6, the shop.** Build the product pages to match the broadsheet. Publish shipping, returns and the 14-day withdrawal terms plainly, including who pays return postage — that disclosure is what keeps the cost off you. Put the GPSR responsible-person details on the pages. Set up order confirmation and dispatch emails from the Zoho mailbox.

**Weeks 7–8, launch quietly.** Open to the Lounge members first — twenty people who already trust you are the ideal first customers and the cheapest possible way to find out what breaks. Fix what breaks.

**Weeks 9–12, connect it to the editorial.** This is the part that actually matters and the part most shops skip. The accessory pages should appear where they are relevant: a cutter on an article about ring gauge, humidity packs on a piece about storage, the travel case on the allowance calculator. Write one honest buying guide — not a listicle, a real comparison of the cutters you have actually handled — and let it earn search traffic. Then look at the numbers and decide whether this is worth continuing.

---

## 8. What would tell you to stop

Set these now, while you are not emotionally invested.

If after ninety days of live selling you are under roughly ten orders a month, the shop is not converting the audience and the answer is either better product choice or no shop — not more listings. If your contribution per order after all of section 3's costs is under about €8, the economics do not survive any advertising spend and the shop only works as a service to existing readers, which is a legitimate thing to decide. If returns and customer service are eating more than a few hours a week at that volume, the catalogue is too broad or the goods are too poor. And if a compliance obligation turns out to be genuinely expensive — the lighter dangerous-goods route is the likely candidate — drop that category rather than the business.
